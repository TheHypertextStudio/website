A consumer terms-of-service document is updated five or six times a year, on average, and read in full by approximately none of the people who agree to the update. The legal community has long known this[^bakos], and the consumer-rights community has long objected to it, but the design community has done relatively little of substance about it. Termsly is the studio's attempt — small in scope, narrow in claim — to do something about one of the more fixable parts of the problem: the part where a consumer cannot tell what changed in a new version of a document they already disagreed with by clicking through.

This study describes the feature inside Termsly that is doing most of the work toward that goal: the redline view. It is, on the surface, a diff. It is, structurally, a small piece of legal-technical inference, because consumer terms documents are not edited between versions. They are rewritten.

## A reader's problem and a writer's problem

Termsly works two halves of one task. The reader's half is a tool that turns a software agreement into language a person can decide on — plain summaries, structured presentation, links between clauses and the user data they affect. The writer's half is a tool that helps a product team draft and publish agreements that are honest about what they ask of the reader. The two halves are built against the same internal model of a clause and share most of their machinery. The redline view is one of the places they meet.

<Marginalia label="def">
  *Redline.* A presentation of a document that visually marks what has changed relative to a prior
  version. The term comes from physical markup in proofreading; the practice predates word
  processing.
</Marginalia>

A redline view shows what changed in a terms document relative to its previous version. Any decent legal team can produce one when the document is being edited in place — Microsoft Word's _Track Changes_ feature is the canonical example. Most consumer terms documents, however, are not edited in place. They are rewritten — sometimes paragraph by paragraph, sometimes section by section, sometimes whole-cloth — and shipped under a fresh `revision-date`. A line-by-line diff between two such versions is mostly noise: every paragraph reads as both deleted and added, with no indication of which differences carry legal weight.

The job of Termsly's redline view is to compute a diff that is useful when the document was rewritten between snapshots. That diff has to answer one question, and the answer must be defensible: _what did this document ask of the reader yesterday, and what does it ask of the reader today?_ That is a question about meaning, not characters. Most of the design budget went there.

## Why line diffs fail on rewritten documents

Two documents may share no consecutive sentence and yet be the same contract. Two documents may share most of their sentences and yet have a single material change buried in a definition. A character-level diff finds the first kind of similarity (it doesn't) and misses the second kind of difference (it can't see it).

The two top clauses in Figure 1 differ at the token level — discretion is named differently, ordering changes — but reserve the same right (terminate without cause, without notice). They are paraphrases of one legal effect. The two bottom clauses are nearly token-identical — same subject, same verb, same object — but reserve different rights. The first requires cause; the second does not. A reader has been promised something different in v2 than in v1.

A line diff applied to either pair would render every word that changed in red and every word that stayed in black, and would produce in the reader exactly the impression that any reader of click-through agreements would expect: nothing distinguishable, attention exhausted, accept-anyway. That impression is the failure mode the redline is designed to short-circuit.

## Anchoring across paraphrase

Termsly's redline machinery is clause-shaped. The parser breaks a document into clauses — the smallest contiguous spans of legal intent, roughly _what the document is asking for in this place_. A limitation-of-liability clause is one anchor. A data-sharing-with-affiliates clause is another. A right-to-delete-an-account clause is a third. The parser is conservative: it splits more often than less and prefers extra anchors to merged ones, on the theory that under-segmentation lets material changes hide inside larger spans, while over-segmentation only costs reading time.

<Marginalia label="impl">
  Clause segmentation is rule-based, not learned. The grammar covers about 92% of consumer terms in
  the studio's evaluation corpus; the rest fall back to paragraph-level segmentation with a flag for
  human review.
</Marginalia>

Each anchor is represented by a normalized statement of intent. That statement is generated from the source clause by an extraction model trained against a hand-labelled corpus of paired clauses, where each pair consists of two paraphrases of the same legal effect with their shared statement written by a lawyer. The statement is short: a single sentence, declarative, naming the subject and the action. Examples:

- _"Affiliates may receive personal data for marketing."_
- _"The provider disclaims liability above the amount paid in the prior twelve months."_
- _"The account holder may delete the account at any time and the provider must honor the deletion within thirty days."_

When two versions of a document are compared, anchors in the new version are matched to anchors in the old version by the similarity of their statements. A high-similarity pair, with no token-level changes in the underlying clause that change the statement, is **unchanged**. A high-similarity pair with token-level edits that do not move the statement is **paraphrased**. A pair where the statements differ in any respect that the classifier flags as legally meaningful is a **material change**. An anchor in the new version with no high-similarity counterpart in the old version is **new**.

Each match is shown to the reader with a short explanation of what the change does, in plain language, and a link to the underlying clause-pair. Reviewers (initially studio staff; eventually a small public review pool) flag missed material changes and over-flagged cosmetic ones, and the misses go back into the training corpus. The classifier improves slowly. It is not allowed to improve quickly: a redline view produced by a fast-moving classifier is harder to trust than one produced by a slow-moving one, because trust in a comparison tool is partly trust that the tool is the same tool today as it was last week. Termsly publishes the classifier's version alongside every redline so a reader who cares can pin against a snapshot.

## Material change versus cosmetic change

This is the most consequential classification call the system makes. The same clause can be reworded into a hundred shapes, ninety-nine of which carry the same legal effect and one of which does not, and the redline is useless if it cannot tell those apart.

The studio's working definition: a **material** change is any change that alters a right, an obligation, a remedy, a data flow, a price, or the conditions under which any of those can change in the future. A **cosmetic** change is any change that does not. The definition is not rigorous in the legal-philosophy sense — material change of contract is a term of art with a body of caselaw the studio is not equipped to argue against. The studio's working definition is operational: it is the rule the classifier uses to decide what to surface to a reader.

Two examples make the line clear. Replacing _we may, in our discretion, terminate this agreement_ with _we may terminate this agreement at any time_ is cosmetic. The discretion that the first phrasing reserves is the same discretion the second phrasing names more directly. Replacing _we may terminate this agreement for cause_ with _we may terminate this agreement at any time_, however, is material. The first phrasing requires cause; the second does not. The words are nearly identical; the rights are not.

<Marginalia label="rule">
  When the classifier is uncertain — a confidence score in a defined middle band — it labels the
  change material and surfaces a "review" badge. The reader still gets to see the difference; the
  badge tells them the system thinks the case is borderline.
</Marginalia>

The rule for ambiguity is to escalate to material. Under-flagging — calling a material change cosmetic — is a worse failure than over-flagging, because the cost of the second falls on a reader who reads a little more carefully and the cost of the first falls on a reader who consents to something they did not understand they were consenting to. The redline view is allowed to be over-cautious. It is not allowed to be quiet.

## What a redline is for

The studio is careful about the language here, because the failure mode of consent UX in this category is exactly the move that a redline view, badly framed, can encourage: the move from _I read what changed and decided_ to _I was shown what changed, so I am bound by it._ The first is consent. The second is a legal fiction.

<PullQuote cite="working note, internal">
  Disclosure is not consent. It is the precondition for consent.
</PullQuote>

What a redline view is for is _informational scaffolding_. It is the instrument that makes consent possible — by reducing the cost of finding out what changed from the cost of reading two full documents to the cost of reading a list — but it does not, by itself, do the consenting. The reader still has to read the list. The reader still has to understand what each item asks. The reader still has to decide. Termsly's job is to keep that decision available to a person who has nine other obligations on a Tuesday afternoon. Termsly's job is not to make the decision on the reader's behalf.

The writer's half of Termsly is built against the same model and asks the writer to mark each material change with a one-sentence explanation in plain language at the time of editing. That explanation appears alongside the redlined clause in the reader's view. The two sides of the tool meet there: the writer says, in plain language, what changed and why; the reader sees the redline and the writer's explanation together. Both sides of an honest agreement are participating in the same document.

## What ships next

The redline view, in its first release, compares two snapshots of the same document. The next release adds two operations the studio has been holding back deliberately, because each one introduces a new failure mode the studio wants to handle carefully.

The first is **multi-version comparison.** A reader who hasn't visited a service in two years should be able to see the cumulative redline of every change since their last visit, not just the most recent revision. The technical work is small; the design challenge is presentational — a redline of fifteen revisions can swamp a reader the same way the underlying document does. The first sketch the studio is testing is a layered presentation: the cumulative summary first, with each individual revision available behind a disclosure.

The second is **cross-provider comparison.** A user evaluating two services with similar offerings should be able to compare their terms documents side-by-side, with anchored matches across providers. This is the same anchor matcher applied to different documents rather than different versions of the same one — the underlying machinery doesn't change, but the interpretation does. Comparing two providers' termination clauses raises the same anti-marketing concern that the rest of Termsly works against: the goal is to inform a reader, not to advise them which service to choose. The presentation will be deliberately neutral — differences highlighted, no recommendations, no scoring.

Both are useful. Neither is the locked delay.
