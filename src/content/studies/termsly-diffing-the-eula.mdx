## A reader's problem and a writer's problem

Termsly works two halves of one task. The reader's half is a tool
that turns a software agreement into language a person can decide on.
The writer's half is a tool that helps a product team draft and
publish agreements that are honest about what they ask of the
reader. The two halves are built against the same internal model of
a clause and share most of their machinery; the redline view is one
of the places they meet.

A redline view shows what changed in a terms-of-service document
relative to its previous version. Any decent legal team can produce
one when the document is being edited in place. Most consumer terms
documents, however, are not edited in place. They are rewritten —
sometimes paragraph by paragraph, sometimes section by section,
sometimes whole-cloth — and shipped under a fresh `revision-date`. A
line-by-line diff between two such versions is mostly noise. The
job of Termsly's redline view is to compute a diff that is useful
when the document was rewritten between snapshots.

## What "diff" means when the document is rewritten

A useful redline answers a single question: _what did this document
ask of the reader yesterday, and what does it ask of the reader
today?_ That is a question about meaning, not about characters.

Two documents may share no consecutive sentence and yet be the same
contract. Two documents may share most of their sentences and yet
have a single material change buried in a definition. A
character-level diff finds the first kind of similarity and misses
the second. A meaning-level diff is what the redline view is trying
to be.

This is the technical heart of the feature, and most of the design
budget went here.

## Anchoring across paraphrase

The internal model is clause-shaped. Termsly's parser breaks a
document into clauses — the smallest contiguous spans of legal
intent, roughly _what the document is asking for in this place_. A
limitation-of-liability clause is one anchor. A data-sharing-with-
affiliates clause is another. A right-to-delete-an-account clause
is a third. The parser is conservative; it splits more often than
less and prefers extra anchors to merged ones.

Each anchor is represented by a normalized statement of intent.
That statement is generated from the source clause by an extraction
model trained against a hand-labelled corpus of paired clauses,
where each pair consists of two paraphrases of the same legal
effect with their shared statement written by a lawyer. The
statement is short — a single sentence, declarative, naming the
subject and the action. "Affiliates may receive personal data for
marketing." "The provider disclaims liability above the amount paid
in the prior twelve months." "The account holder may delete the
account at any time and the provider must honor the deletion within
thirty days."

When two versions of a document are compared, anchors in the new
version are matched to anchors in the old version by the similarity
of their statements. A high-similarity pair, with no token-level
changes in the underlying clause that change the statement, is
_unchanged_. A high-similarity pair with token-level edits that do
not move the statement is _paraphrased_. A pair where the statements
differ in any respect that the classifier flags as legally
meaningful is a _material change_. An anchor in the new version
with no high-similarity counterpart in the old version is _new_.

Each match is shown to the reader with a short explanation.
Reviewers flag misses; the misses go back into the training corpus.
The classifier improves slowly; it is not allowed to improve
quickly, because a redline produced by a fast-moving classifier is
harder to trust than a redline produced by a slow-moving one.

## Material change versus cosmetic change

This is the most consequential classification call. The same clause
can be reworded into a hundred shapes, ninety-nine of which carry
the same legal effect and one of which does not, and the redline is
useless if it cannot tell those apart.

The studio's working definition: a _material_ change is any change
that alters a right, an obligation, a remedy, a data flow, a price,
or the conditions under which any of those can change in the
future. A _cosmetic_ change is any change that does not.

Two examples make the line clear. Replacing _we may, in our
discretion, terminate this agreement_ with _we may terminate this
agreement at any time_ is cosmetic. The discretion that the first
phrasing reserves is the same discretion the second phrasing names
more directly. Replacing _we may terminate this agreement for cause_
with _we may terminate this agreement at any time_, however, is
material. The first phrasing requires cause; the second does not.
The words are nearly identical; the rights are not.

The rule for ambiguity is to escalate to material. Under-flagging
(calling a material change cosmetic) is a worse failure than
over-flagging (calling a cosmetic change material), because the
cost of the second falls on a reader who reads a little more
carefully and the cost of the first falls on a reader who consents
to something they did not understand they were consenting to. The
redline view is allowed to be over-cautious. It is not allowed to
be quiet.

## What a redline is for

A redline view is not consent. The studio is careful with this
language because the failure mode of consent UX in this category is
exactly the move that a redline view, badly framed, can encourage:
the move from _I read what changed and decided_ to _I was shown
what changed, so I am bound by it_. The first is consent. The
second is a legal fiction.

What a redline view is for is _informational scaffolding_. It is
the instrument that makes consent possible — by reducing the cost
of finding out what changed from the cost of reading two full
documents to the cost of reading a list — but it does not, by
itself, do the consenting. The reader still has to read the list.
The reader still has to understand what each item asks. The reader
still has to decide. Termsly's job is to keep that decision
available to a person who has nine other obligations on a Tuesday
afternoon. Termsly's job is not to make the decision on the
reader's behalf.

The writer's half of Termsly is built against the same model and
asks the writer to mark each material change with a one-sentence
explanation in plain language at the time of editing. That
explanation appears alongside the redlined clause in the reader's
view. The two sides of the tool meet there: the writer says, in
plain language, what changed and why; the reader sees the redline
and the writer's explanation together. Both sides of an honest
agreement are participating in the same document.

That is the feature. The rest is upkeep.
