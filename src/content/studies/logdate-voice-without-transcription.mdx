## The decision

LogDate captures voice. Voice stays voice. There is no transcript pane,
no searchable text rendering of what was said, no AI summary at the top
of the entry. Audio entries are stored, played back, and revisited as
audio.

This is the decision. The rest of the study is about what it costs and
what it preserves, and why the studio decided that the second of those
was worth the first.

The default in this category is the opposite. Most journaling apps that
accept voice immediately turn voice into text — for search, for
"intelligence", for the convenience of the eye. The transcript becomes
the canonical artifact; the audio becomes a redundant copy. Within a few
months, most users stop listening to their own recordings entirely. The
voice is reduced to data about itself.

LogDate's working hypothesis is that this reduction loses something the
software is supposed to be holding.

## What voice does that text cannot

A voice carries information that text does not. Hesitation, register,
pace, the texture of a particular morning. When a person reads their own
journal entry a year later, they read what they wrote and infer the
rest. When they listen, they hear it.

Voicemail is the closest commercial precedent worth naming. The
voicemail left by a parent ten years ago, kept on a phone that has since
changed twice, is not retained because of its information content. It
is retained because of the voice. The same logic applies to a journal
entry recorded on the morning of a difficult day. The transcript would
say what was happening. The recording says how it sounded.

Walter Ong's distinction between primary and secondary orality is useful
here without being decorative. Primary orality is speech in cultures
without writing — speech as the only medium. Secondary orality is
speech mediated by, and at home alongside, written technology — phone
calls, podcasts, voice memos. LogDate's audio entries are deliberately
secondary orality: written-down at the level of the system (the entry
is dated, indexed, navigable), but not at the level of the speech
itself. The thing spoken stays spoken.

## What the absence of transcription protects

Three protections are what the decision earns, named here in the order
they were considered.

The first is the privacy of half-formed thought. A transcript reads
like a finished document. A recording does not. People who know they
are being transcribed self-edit before they speak, and self-edit again
when they look at the transcript. Both edits subtract from what the
journal is for. A recording asks a different version of the speaker —
the one willing to think out loud — and does not later confront that
speaker with their unfinished sentences laid out in print.

The second is the speaker's relationship to their own voice over time.
Returning to a year-old entry as audio is qualitatively different from
returning to it as text. The voice is changed; the listener is changed;
the pair of those things is what the experience of remembrance is. A
transcript flattens that pair into a single line of words.

The third is more architectural than personal. Without transcription,
LogDate's servers do not contain a searchable corpus of user thought.
Nothing on the back end is text-indexable. There is no pile of
journal-text waiting to be analyzed, profiled, or, in a worst case,
subpoenaed. Audio in encrypted blobs is harder to misuse than text in
rows. The studio considers this a feature, and one of the easier ones
to maintain over time.

## How the entries find their way back

A journal that is never re-read is not a journal. Voice without
transcription puts more pressure on the navigation surface than a
text-based system would, and LogDate spends most of its design budget
there.

Entries are organized by date. The home surface is a calendar; the
secondary surface is a thin index of dates with audio attached. There
is no chronological feed in the social-media sense. There is no "for
you" view. The system surfaces entries on calendar anniversaries — the
same date last year, the same date five years ago — and surfaces
nothing else without an explicit request. The reasoning is that the
units of memory people return to voluntarily are dates, places, and
people; LogDate knows the first reliably, the second imperfectly, and
the third not at all, so it builds the surface around dates and adds
nothing it cannot ground.

The audio itself lives locally. A recorded entry is encrypted on
device before it is written to disk; the key lives in the device's
secure enclave; the cloud sync layer carries opaque blobs. No
third-party transcription service ever sees the audio, because there
is no third-party transcription service in the loop. There is no
first-party one either.

This is mentioned because it is operationally tedious. The studio
could add transcription tomorrow and many users would consider it an
improvement. The decision not to is not a default; it is a position.
The position is that the absence is what the software is for.

## What the experiment so far has taught

Two observations from the first year of use, written here as
observations and not as conclusions.

People return to old audio entries less often than they return to old
text entries. The number is consistent across cohorts and not
particularly close. A text entry is two seconds to scan; an audio
entry is its full duration to revisit. The friction is real, and it
disadvantages audio under any naive measure of engagement. LogDate
does not optimize for engagement.

The audio entries that people do return to are described, in the
small qualitative review the studio runs once per release, as feeling
more present than the text entries returned to alongside them.
Several people used the same word: _vivid_. None used it for text.
This is the part of the result the studio takes seriously.

A journal is for the small number of moments worth returning to.
Audio loses on the volume-of-revisit metric and wins on the
value-per-revisit one. The studio is comfortable with that
trade-off. The next release will not change it.
