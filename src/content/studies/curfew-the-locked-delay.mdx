## The primitive

Curfew has one design idea that does the load-bearing work and several
that hold up the edges. The load-bearing one is the locked delay. It
works like this: a rule, once written, can be changed only after a
delay the writer chose at the moment of writing. If the delay is
fifteen minutes, the next edit takes fifteen minutes to apply. If the
delay is twenty-four hours, edits take twenty-four hours.

Read out, the rule says: _the version of the user that wrote the rule
outvotes the version that would change it_. The locked delay is a way
of letting the calmer self bind the less calm self, in writing, in
advance, with the device as the impartial third party.

This is not a metaphor. It is implemented exactly that way. Curfew
stores rules with two timestamps — the time the rule was set, and the
earliest time at which a pending edit may take effect. The runtime
enforces the later of those two against any request to use a blocked
app. Edits are queued; the queue is honored as soon as its delay
expires; the rule applies until then.

## A short history of the self-binding contract

The locked delay is not new. It is a small re-implementation of an
old human technology, and naming the precedents is part of taking the
feature seriously.

Odysseus and the mast is the canonical case. Odysseus, planning to
sail past the Sirens, asks his crew to bind him to the mast and to
refuse his orders to be released, no matter what he says when the
singing begins. He delegates judgment to a future version of his crew
that is operating under instructions he gave when his judgment was
sound. The software equivalent is a rule he could not change for the
duration of the passage.

In psychiatric ethics, the term _Ulysses pact_ is used for advance
directives written by people during periods of mental health to
govern their care during periods of crisis — for instance, a person
with bipolar disorder authorizing involuntary admission during a
manic episode, in writing, while euthymic. The legal apparatus is
heavy and the ethics are difficult; the underlying mechanism is the
same as Odysseus's. A self that knows what it will face commits a
future self that will not be able to refuse coherently.

The Christmas-club bank account belongs to a different register
entirely, but the structural shape is identical. A small amount of
money is deposited weekly into an account that pays no meaningful
interest and cannot be withdrawn until December. The account exists
for one reason: to remove from the depositor's reach the option of
spending the money before it has accumulated. The locked delay is a
Christmas-club account for time of attention.

The alarm clock placed across the room is the smallest version. The
sleeper of last night judged that the sleeper of this morning would
be better off standing up to silence the alarm, and arranged the
room so that the morning self has no available shortcut. Curfew's
locked delay is the same gesture, generalized to a wider class of
impulses.

These are named to make the lineage explicit. The studio is not
inventing a primitive; it is implementing one that has been useful
to people for a long time, in a place where it has not previously
been available — the device through which the impulse most often
runs.

## What a delay-lock does in code

The implementation is small, which is intentional. Complexity in the
mechanism would invite complexity in the failure modes, and the
failure modes of an attention instrument matter in a way that the
failure modes of, say, a feed reader do not.

A rule is a record with four fields: the conditions that trigger it
(app identifiers, time windows, location guards), the action it
takes when triggered (block, dim, prompt, quiet), the timestamp at
which the rule was set, and the earliest timestamp at which any
pending change to the rule may apply. Edits to a rule are not edits
in place. They are new versions queued behind a delay; the live
rule is whichever version has crossed its earliest-effective
timestamp.

The runtime that enforces rules is a foreground process that
watches device state and gates app launches. When a rule fires, the
launch is blocked and the user is shown a screen that says, in
plain language, which rule is firing and when it can be changed.
The screen contains no ten-second-skip, no per-app override, no
trick by which the rule can be shortened from the surface that the
rule itself is acting on. Any shortening must come from the rule
editor, which is where the locked delay is enforced.

Clock manipulation is the obvious attack on a delay-based mechanism.
Curfew handles it by anchoring delays to a monotonic clock that
does not rewind across reboots, and by independently reading time
from a trusted network source on every rule edit. A device whose
clock has been moved forward will simply not see its rules unlock
until the real time has caught up. This is unglamorous and
necessary; without it the locked delay would be a suggestion.

## The cases where a delay is not enough

Three honest cases where the primitive is wrong on its own, named
with what the studio shipped instead.

A rule someone wrote during a calm hour can turn out to be wrong in
a different way. A blocked app may need to be reached during an
emergency; a parent locked out of a messaging app at 11 p.m. may
need to receive an emergency call from a child at 11:30. Curfew's
response is a small, named exception list that the user maintains
_outside_ the locked-delay system: a short list of apps and contacts
that are never blocked, edited under no delay. The exception list
is itself reviewed in the rule editor, and adding to it is a
deliberate, separately journaled action. The point is that
emergency-handling is its own contract, not a hole in the main one.

A rule strict enough to matter can be circumvented by leaving the
device. The studio has no answer to this and does not pretend to.
The person who is willing to put down the phone and read a book is
doing the right thing; the locked delay was not built to stop that.
It was built to stop the smaller move, the half-impulse to pick up
the device that the user did not want to pick up. Anything beyond
that is not software's job.

A rule can name the wrong app. The user wanted to block a different
app and chose this one by mistake; the rule is now locked behind a
twenty-four-hour delay; the wrong app is unrestricted and the right
app is unaffected. Curfew's response here is the dullest one
imaginable: a confirmation step before a rule locks, with a printed
summary of what the rule will do, and a five-second pause before
the lock takes effect. Five seconds is not a meaningful delay
against impulse, but it is a meaningful delay against typo.

## What we think we are building

Curfew is not a behavior-change tool. It does not promise that
anyone will spend less time on their phone, that anyone will read
more, that anyone will sleep better. Behavior-change products in
the attention space have a long history of overpromising on
outcomes and underdelivering on the underlying primitive. The
studio's approach is the reverse: deliver one well-built primitive,
name what it does and what it does not do, and let the outcomes
accrue or not on their own terms.

What Curfew is, instead, is a consent instrument. It allows a
person to consent in advance to constraints they cannot yet feel
the need for, and to commit to those constraints in a form the
device can hold. Whether a person uses the instrument well is a
different question and not one software can settle. Whether the
instrument exists at all has been, until now, the missing piece.
