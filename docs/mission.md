# Hypertext Studio — Mission and Posture

This document is the studio's constitution. It describes what Hypertext Studio is, what it inherits from, what it refuses to be, and how its work — including its website — should feel.

It is intended to be read alongside the build specification (`hypertext-studio-spec.md`). The spec answers _how_ to build the website. This document answers _what the studio is for_. When the spec leaves a decision open, this document is the tiebreaker.

If anything produced for the studio reads as a startup pitch, a SaaS landing page, an agency portfolio, a venture-funded growth surface, or a generic "design engineer" microsite, it has departed from the mission. Return here.

---

## 1. What Hypertext Studio is

Hypertext Studio is a small design lab building sustainable, human-centered software. We build digital experiences that connect people. In practice, that means websites and software — and for us, the two are sides of the same coin. A website is software with a public face; software is a website with state. We treat both as the same craft.

We are an LLC and a Public Benefit Corporation. We are not venture-scale. We do not have investors, a runway, or a growth curve we are optimizing. We make money — the products we ship are real, paid software with real users — but the purpose of the studio is the research, not the revenue. Revenue funds the next question. It is not the goal.

We are design engineers. We work close to the materials: HTML, CSS, native platform APIs, real production code, real users. We do not stop at decks. The artifact is the product.

## 2. The fourth mode

A design practice usually operates in one of three modes:

1. **Client work**, where the idea is constrained by what a client will pay for. The idea is real but compromised.
2. **Research and prototyping**, where the idea is unconstrained but never tested at scale. Honest experiments that don't have to face users who didn't sign up for the thesis.
3. **Product company**, where the idea bends toward growth, retention, and whatever else the business needs. There is only one design question, and it is "how do we grow this."

We work in a fourth mode: **design research where the apparatus is a real product.**

Each product is a sustained attempt to answer one design question. The product must work as a product first — it pays for itself, supports its users, deals with the App Store, the support tickets, the accessibility audits, the people using the software in ways the designer did not predict. The research happens _through_ that constraint, not despite it.

Design ideas are not real until they have survived users who did not sign up for the manifesto.

## 3. The lineage

The studio's name is not decorative. "Hypertext" gestures past the web-as-it-is and toward the web-as-it-was-supposed-to-be:

- Vannevar Bush's Memex (1945)
- Ted Nelson's Xanadu and the original definition of hypertext (1963)
- Doug Engelbart's NLS and the Mother of All Demos (1968)
- Tim Berners-Lee's first browser and the original info.cern.ch (1990)
- HyperCard (1987) — hypertext for the masses
- Bret Victor's interactive explanations and Dynamicland

These are not nostalgic references. They are unfinished work.

The mainstream web kept the cheapest technical part of hypertext — one-way URLs — and dropped the richer parts: bidirectionality, fine-grained addressability, two-way authorship, persistent versioning, and the **social-contract layer** that Nelson cared about most. Who gets credit. What persists. How consent and authorship work. What the contract between author and reader is.

The studio is recovering that abandoned layer in the form of products. Each thing we ship is, in some way, an attempt at finishing a piece of work that was started decades ago.

### Contemporary fellow travelers

- **Ink & Switch** — the closest peer in posture and editorial sensibility. Independent research lab, design-engineering ethos, ships essays alongside prototypes. We diverge in one direction: they research-and-prototype; we research-and-ship.
- **Are.na** — small, opinionated, anti-extraction, deliberately not venture-scale, sustains itself on subscriptions. The cultural posture is the closest contemporary analog to ours.
- **Teenage Engineering** — small operation, fund-the-lab-with-products model. Software equivalent does not currently exist. We are trying to be one.
- **The Browser Company** — small team treating the browser-as-software-medium as a design problem, with a strong editorial voice. We share the editorial register; we don't share the venture trajectory.

### Ethical critique we inherit

- **Doc Searls / Project VRM** — vendor relationship management, flipping the user-vendor power dynamic. Termsly is downstream of this whether the lineage is named or not.
- **Center for Humane Technology** (Tristan Harris and others) — the critique that produced the conditions for Curfew.
- **ToSDR** (Terms of Service; Didn't Read), **Mozilla's Privacy Not Included** — direct prior art for Termsly's mission.

We inherit from these movements even when we do not cite them in copy. Anyone working on the studio's brand, voice, or website should know they exist.

## 4. The current experiments

Three products in flight. **They are conceptually independent.** They are not a thematic suite. They are not layers in a stack. They share infrastructure where it makes sense; they answer different questions.

### LogDate

**The question:** What does personal memory software look like when it isn't designed for surveillance, feed extraction, or social validation?

**The tagline:** _A better home to document and store your memories._

### Curfew

**The question:** What does it look like to give people real, hard-edged control over their own attention — including the ability to revoke their own consent to their devices, before the impulse to use them arises?

**The tagline:** _Software for the version of you that planned ahead._

### Termsly

**The question:** What would informed consent actually require, in UX terms, between digital services and the people who use them?

**The tagline:** _Know what you're agreeing to._

Each is the apparatus for one question. Each must work as a product. Each will produce **studies** — design research artifacts written alongside major releases, eventually structured as Distill.pub– and Pudding.cool–style interactive essays — that document what the experiment taught the field.

The studies are the studio's intellectual contribution. The products are the apparatus.

## 5. The publishing model

Two artifact types:

1. **Products.** Continuous, supported, in production. The thing real users use.
2. **Studies.** Design research write-ups tied to major feature releases — not portfolio shots, but real research papers. Each study describes a design question, the bets we made, what users taught us, what worked, what didn't, and what the experiment ultimately said about the question.

Each product gets a **launch study** when it first ships and a **drop study** for each subsequent major feature release. Combined, the studies form the studio's documentary trail — a record of design ideas being tested in production.

Publishing is **POSSE** — Publish on Own Site, Syndicate Elsewhere. The canonical artifact lives on `hypertext.studio`. Copies appear on Bluesky, the fediverse (via Bridgy Fed), and anywhere else they're useful, all pointing home. We do not publish first on someone else's platform.

## 6. What we are NOT

The studio's identity is partly defined by what it refuses to be. The following list is exhaustive enough to settle most ambiguous decisions:

- **Not a venture-scale startup.** No growth curve, no runway, no investors, no pitch deck, no metrics dashboard for the studio itself. Individual products may have business metrics; the studio does not.
- **Not an agency or consultancy.** We do not take clients. We do not produce decks. We do not invoice. The artifacts of our work are products and studies, not contracts.
- **Not a research lab that doesn't ship.** Ink & Switch's posture without their patron model. Prototypes that never face users teach us less than products that do.
- **Not a generative-AI-interface lab.** Generative UI work happens at Reasonable Tech Company on the Lovelace platform — wholly separate. The studio's products are not vehicles for AI experimentation.
- **Not a portfolio site disguised as a studio.** Our products are not "case studies in someone else's project." They are sustained, supported, real software.
- **Not Vercel, Linear, Raycast, or the broader design-engineer milieu in posture.** We share craft sensibility with that scene; we do not share its venture trajectory or its visual conventions.
- **Not a media company, content brand, or thought-leadership operation.** The studies are research write-ups, not blog posts optimized for engagement.
- **Not a community or a movement.** We are an organization that makes things.

## 7. Voice

Calm. Declarative. Considered.

- We do not chirp. We do not pitch. We do not power-word.
- We avoid superlatives, urgency, manufactured FOMO, and the entire vocabulary of growth marketing.
- We write in plain language. We do not perform humility ("we're just a small team trying to make a difference") and we do not perform expertise ("revolutionizing the future of…"). We state what we are and let the reader judge.
- We use real lists with supporting prose, not bullet-point soup. We vary sentence length deliberately. We use depth where it earns its place, brevity where it doesn't.
- We do not write copy that reads like AI wrote it: no airy abstractions, no hedge-word spirals, no "in today's fast-paced world."
- When in doubt, write the way a thoughtful person would write a letter to someone they respect.

## 8. The website's posture

The studio's website is a public face for the studio's identity. It must read as a thoughtful artifact, not a marketing surface.

### Concrete posture rules

The site is **a document, not an app.** Semantic HTML, real headings, real lists, real anchor links, real `<dl>` metadata. The page is structured the way a research paper or a small chapbook is structured. View-source is part of the design — anyone who runs it should find a clean, hand-crafted page they could learn from.

The site **uses the web's native primitives.** `<dialog>`, `popover`, `:has()`, View Transitions, microformats, native focus rings restyled but not removed. We reach for the web's own surface area before reaching for npm.

The site **rewards close attention without performing for it.** Microcopy, hover reveals, keyboard shortcuts, the colophon-as-source, the DNS TXT poem in the footer — these are craft details, not easter eggs in the marketing sense. The reader who looks finds something thoughtful.

The site **takes notes from the past to inform the future.** It revives early-web affordances the modern web abandoned: the status bar showing destination URLs on link hover, link colors that mean something (visited links fade), view-source as pedagogy, marginalia, definition lists, browser-default tooltips. It avoids kitsch nostalgia — no marquee tags, no "under construction" gifs, no Geocities pastiche.

The site **is calm before it is clever.** The dominant register is calm and document-y. Playfulness lives in small moments, not in the dominant impression.

The site **is finite.** It fits in a few scrolls. It ends. There is no infinite scroll, no lazy-loaded "discover more" sections, no scroll-jacking. The footer is the closing scene of a small press book.

## 9. Anti-patterns — what NOT to produce

Concrete examples of patterns the studio's website must avoid. If a draft contains any of these, return to this document.

### Visual / structural anti-patterns

- A SaaS-style hero section: full-viewport gradient block, display text used as a billboard, a primary CTA button labeled "Get started" / "Try it free" / "Sign up" / "Book a demo," and a phone-with-app mockup floating against the gradient.
- A "Trusted by" logo strip with company logos.
- A bento grid of features. Bento grids belong on dashboard products; this site is not a dashboard.
- A pricing tier comparison ladder for the studio (individual products may have these on their own sites — the studio's site does not).
- A "Built for teams that ship" headline or any variant thereof.
- An "Our process" diagram with three to five steps and abstract icons.
- A scrolling testimonials carousel. There are no testimonials of the studio.
- A loop of an in-app demo video as a hero element.
- An "Our values" section with iconographic cards.
- A "Manifesto" page that is actually a copywriter's expansion of the homepage thesis.
- A blog index labeled "Insights" or "Resources" or "Thinking."
- A footer with a newsletter signup. Subscription happens via RSS / Atom / JSON Feed, fediverse, and Bluesky — not by email capture.
- An exit-intent modal. Ever.
- A cookie banner. The site sets no cookies; nothing to consent to.
- A "Start your free trial" anywhere on the studio site.
- A "Powered by" banner.
- An auto-playing video, anywhere.
- "AI-powered" copy describing the studio or its work. The studio is not an AI brand. (Reasonable Tech is.)

### Copy anti-patterns

- "Empowering designers to ship better products."
- "Reimagining the future of [anything]."
- "Building the next generation of [anything]."
- "We help [audience] [verb] [outcome]."
- "Trusted by [number] of teams worldwide."
- "Join thousands of designers already using…"
- "Coming soon" countdowns, "Get early access" gates, "Limited spots remaining" urgency.
- "In today's fast-paced world…" or any opener that gestures at a worldview before saying anything.
- "Our mission is to…" framings followed by abstract values. The mission is the work. Show the work.
- The word "innovative." The word "seamless." The word "disrupting." The word "leverage." The word "robust." The word "powerful." The word "intuitive." The phrase "best-in-class." Any of these flag a draft for rewriting.
- Sentences that hedge ("We believe that perhaps software might…"). Declare or do not include.
- AI-prose patterns: tricolons of empty abstractions, paragraphs that say the same thing three different ways, sentences that begin "It's worth noting that…" or "Importantly,…"

### Tone anti-patterns

- Founder-style first-person performance ("I'm Willie, and I started this because…").
- Performative humility ("we're just a small team…").
- Performative expertise ("after 15 years in the industry…").
- Cheekiness in places that should be calm. Save playfulness for the footer marquee, the colophon, and the DNS TXT poem.
- Cheekiness in places that should be plain. The privacy page is plain. The microcopy on functional buttons is plain. The error states are plain.

### Visual character anti-patterns

- Bouncy hover animations.
- Gradient backgrounds outside of the OG card.
- Drop shadows that suggest panel depth.
- Cards in the modern UI sense — rounded boxes with shadows on a flat background.
- Display sans for body copy. The body face is a serif (Source Serif 4). The display face is sans (Inter). Reversing this destroys the document feel.
- Pure white backgrounds. The site is on cream.
- Generic system focus rings. The focus ring is restyled to fit the document.
- Custom cursors. Honor the platform.
- Smooth-scroll snap or scroll-jacking effects.
- Parallax.
- Particle effects, mesh gradients, animated SVG backgrounds.

## 10. Reference register

When in doubt, the site should read like:

- A page from an Ink & Switch report.
- A chapter of a Stripe Press book.
- A long letter from someone you respect.
- The colophon of a small independent magazine.
- A research paper that happens to live on the web.
- The first chapter of a small hand-bound book.
- A typeset essay by Robin Sloan or Maggie Appleton.

It should not read like:

- A Vercel landing page.
- A Linear marketing site.
- A Webflow template.
- A Mailchimp newsletter.
- A Figma file shared as a portfolio.
- A Y Combinator startup's "Show HN" launch post.
- A Dribbble shot.
- A Notion-built homepage with emoji headers.
- An AI-generated SaaS template.

## 11. The questions to ask, in order, when uncertain

1. **Would this belong in an Ink & Switch report, a Stripe Press chapter, or a small hand-bound book?** If no, it doesn't belong here.
2. **Does this make the page louder or calmer?** Default to calmer.
3. **Is this for the careful reader, or for the bounce metric?** Build for the careful reader.
4. **Are we performing depth or actually being deep?** Be deep, don't perform.
5. **Would Ted Nelson recognize this as continuous with his project?** If not, reconsider.
6. **Could this sentence have been written by an AI optimizing for content marketing?** If yes, rewrite it as a person would.
7. **Is this clever for the reader, or clever for us?** Cleverness in service of the reader is craft. Cleverness in service of the studio's image is vanity.

## 12. How to use this document

Read it before opening the build spec. Re-read it whenever a draft starts to feel off-key. Keep it open while making decisions about copy, layout, voice, or visual direction.

The build spec describes the surface of the work. This document describes the soul of it. Both are needed. Neither is sufficient alone.

If a build decision conflicts between the two, the spec gives way. Posture wins.
